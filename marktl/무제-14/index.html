Ferris Wheel Confinement, Negligence, and NIED

사고 직후 관람차에 갇힌 아동, 아무 조치를 하지 않은 직원, 그리고 사고를 직접 보지 못한 임신한 어머니. 이 문제는 불법감금, 놀이공원 과실, 그리고 방관자형 정신적 고통 손해배상의 경계를 한 번에 묻는다.

Social-friendly framing

A jury could find Funworld liable for falsely imprisoning Paul and for Employee’s negligent failure to stop the rocking car, but Mom’s NIED claim likely fails because she did not contemporaneously perceive Paul’s injury.

Abstract

이 보고서는 NCBE 스타일의 불법행위 문제를 공개 아티클 형식으로 재구성한 것이다. 핵심 질문은 세 가지다. 첫째, 사고 후 직원이 관람차를 재가동하지 않아 Paul을 차량 안에 30분간 머물게 한 것이 불법감금인지. 둘째, 직원이 여섯 살 아이들이 차량을 격하게 흔드는 것을 보고도 제지하지 않은 것이 과실인지. 셋째, 사고를 직접 목격하지 못한 Mom이 정신적 고통 및 그로 인한 유산에 대해 손해배상을 받을 수 있는지다.

결론은 선명하다. Paul의 불법감금 청구와 Funworld의 과실 책임은 배심원이 인정할 수 있다. 반면 Mom의 NIED 청구는 법적으로 취약하다. 그녀는 Paul과 가까운 친족이고 실제 신체적 결과도 겪었지만, 사고 당시 위험구역 안에 있지 않았고 Paul의 부상을 감각적으로, 동시적으로 목격하지도 않았다.

Key Findings

Finding 1

False imprisonment: likely yes

Paul은 자발적으로 탑승했지만, 사고 후 내려달라고 요청했다. 직원이 설명 없이 30분간 재가동을 거부한 순간, 초기 동의의 범위를 넘어선 의도적 구금으로 평가될 수 있다.

Finding 2

Negligence: likely yes

차량을 격하게 흔드는 행위는 발생 가능성이 낮더라도 피해 규모가 크다. 아이들에게 멈추라고 지시하는 부담은 작아, 합리적 배심원은 직원의 무대응을 주의의무 위반으로 볼 수 있다.

Finding 3

NIED: likely no

Mom은 사고를 직접 보지 못했고, 100야드 밖에 있었으며, 경보를 듣고 나중에 도착했다. 유산이라는 중대한 결과가 있어도 방관자형 NIED의 문턱 요건을 충족하지 못한다.

Factual Frame

Funworld에서 여덟 살 Paul은 관람차에 탑승했고, 임신 중이던 Mom은 피곤하여 약 100야드 떨어진 곳에서 기다렸다. Employee는 Paul의 안전바를 고정했다. 관람차가 움직이자 Paul은 뒤 차량에 탄 여섯 살 남자아이 둘의 비명과 소란을 들었다. Employee도 그들이 차량을 격하게 흔드는 것을 보고 들었지만 아무 조치를 하지 않았다.

Paul의 차량이 꼭대기에서 내려오기 시작할 때, 뒤 차량의 아이들이 안전바를 세게 흔들어 잠금이 풀렸다. 두 아이는 추락했고, 그중 한 명은 떨어지며 Paul을 쳤다. Employee는 관람차를 멈추고 비상 경보를 울렸다. Mom은 사고를 보지 못했지만 경보를 듣고 달려왔다. 군중 때문에 Paul을 볼 수 없었고, 한 행인이 “a little boy has been killed”라고 말했다.

추락한 아이들은 10분 뒤 구급차로 이송되었다. Paul과 다른 승객들은 내려달라고 요청했지만 Employee는 설명 없이 관람차를 재가동하지 않았다. 30분 뒤 매니저가 와서야 Employee에게 재가동과 하차를 지시했다. 사고 40분 뒤 Mom은 Paul과 재회했다. Paul은 경미한 부상 치료를 받았고, Mom은 사고 관련 스트레스로 유산했다.

  • Ride begins Paul boards; Employee fastens the safety bar. Mom waits 100 yards away.
  • Accident Two boys rock their car; the safety bar unlatches; both fall; one strikes Paul.
  • Emergency pause Employee stops the wheel and sounds an alarm. Mom arrives but cannot see Paul.
  • 40 minutes later Manager orders restart; passengers exit; Paul and Mom go to the hospital.

Issue 1: Funworld’s False Imprisonment of Paul

NCBE weighting: 40%

Rule

  • False imprisonment requires intent to confine the plaintiff within a bounded area.
  • The defendant’s conduct must cause confinement, or the defendant must fail to release the plaintiff despite a duty to do so.
  • The plaintiff must know of the confinement or be harmed by it.
  • There is no confinement if the plaintiff knows of a reasonable and safe means of escape.

Paul’s strongest claim is not that the ride initially trapped him. He voluntarily entered the Ferris wheel car and accepted the safety restraint. The decisive legal moment came after the emergency response had stabilized. Paul and other passengers begged to be removed, yet Employee refused to restart the wheel for 30 minutes without explanation.

Consent to a ride is not consent to indefinite suspension in midair after the ride operator has a duty to release passengers. Core takeaway for Issue 1

Paul was confined in a limited area: the Ferris wheel car. Because it was suspended above the ground, there was no reasonable safe escape. Paul was conscious of the confinement, as shown by his pleas to be taken off. Employee’s unexplained refusal supports intent: he knew Paul remained confined and chose not to release him.

Funworld’s best defense is that Paul voluntarily submitted to the ride’s ordinary restraint. But that defense weakens once the ride stops after an accident and the child asks to leave. The original consent covered ordinary operation, not an unexplained 30-minute detention after the emergency. Since Funworld concedes Employee acted within the scope of employment, vicarious liability follows.

Conclusion: A jury could properly find that Funworld falsely imprisoned Paul.

Issue 2: Negligence for Failing to Stop the Rocking Car

NCBE weighting: 25%

Rule

  • Negligence requires duty, breach, actual and proximate causation, and damages.
  • Funworld owed Paul, a public invitee, reasonable care against unsafe conditions and unreasonable risks.
  • Reasonable care turns on foreseeable likelihood of harm, foreseeable severity of harm, and burden of precautions.

Funworld’s accident-history evidence helps it, but does not end the negligence analysis. National records showed only one similar injury incident in 40 years. That fact suggests the probability of this exact accident was low. But negligence doctrine does not ask only whether the accident was common. It also asks how serious the harm could be and how easy it would have been to reduce the risk.

Foreseeability Employee saw and heard the two boys screaming and rocking the car vigorously while the Ferris wheel was moving.
Severity A fall from a Ferris wheel car can cause grave injury or death, even if the statistical likelihood is low.
Precaution burden Employee could have instructed the boys to stop, warned them, slowed the ride, or taken other ordinary operator steps.
Causation The rocking led to the safety bar unlatching; the boys fell; one struck Paul, causing minor injuries.

The burden of precaution was small. A simple command to stop rocking, a warning, or operational intervention would have been far less costly than the risk posed by a vigorously rocking elevated car. Because the fall caused Paul’s injury, the causation and damages elements are also present.

Conclusion: A jury could properly find that Funworld was negligent through Employee’s failure to act.

Issue 3: Mom’s NIED Claim and Miscarriage

NCBE weighting: 35%

Rule

  • A plaintiff may recover for NIED if placed within the zone of danger and emotionally distressed by fear of physical impact.
  • Many jurisdictions also permit bystander recovery for close relatives who are present at the scene and contemporaneously perceive the injury.
  • Emotional distress based only on later news, rumor, or uncertainty generally does not satisfy the bystander rule.

Mom’s facts are sympathetic but legally difficult. She was Paul’s mother, she was pregnant, and she suffered a miscarriage after the stress of the incident. The miscarriage supplies a serious physical consequence. But it does not cure the threshold problem: she was not in the zone of danger and did not contemporaneously observe Paul’s injury.

The emotional harm was real; the NIED claim fails because the law limits recovery to plaintiffs who were personally endangered or who directly perceived the injury. Core takeaway for Issue 3

Mom waited about 100 yards away. She did not see the boys fall, did not see one strike Paul, and arrived only after the alarm sounded. Once she arrived, the crowd blocked her view. The bystander’s statement that “a little boy has been killed” intensified her panic, but it was still information received after the accident rather than a sensory and contemporaneous perception of Paul’s injury.

The bystander rule is designed to distinguish direct traumatic perception from later notification or fear. Mom feared that the dead child might be Paul, but the news was not even specifically about Paul. Her distress arose from uncertainty, alarm, and later-discovered facts, not from seeing or otherwise directly perceiving Paul being injured.

Conclusion: Mom likely cannot recover NIED damages, even for the resulting miscarriage.

Implications for Exam Writing

This problem rewards disciplined separation of sympathy from doctrine. Paul’s claims turn on concrete conduct by Employee: failure to release him and failure to stop a visible danger. Mom’s claim, by contrast, turns on doctrinal limits: proximity, direct perception, and the difference between witnessing injury and receiving frightening information.

Writing Move Why It Matters Best Use in This Problem
Identify the timing shift Paul’s consent matters only before the emergency detention becomes unreasonable. Emphasize the 30-minute unexplained refusal after pleas to exit.
Use risk-burden analysis Low accident frequency does not defeat negligence when severity is high and precaution is easy. Compare one prior incident in 40 years with the catastrophic risk of falling from height.
Separate harm from eligibility Mom’s miscarriage proves seriousness, but not the required NIED relationship to the accident. State that the claim fails before damages because she did not observe the injury.

Copy-ready answer frame

A jury could find Funworld liable for false imprisonment because Employee intentionally kept Paul confined in a Ferris wheel car with no safe means of escape after Paul asked to be released. A jury could also find negligence because Employee saw and heard vigorous rocking, the risk of severe harm was foreseeable, and the burden of intervention was slight. Mom’s NIED claim likely fails because she was outside the zone of danger and did not contemporaneously perceive Paul’s injury, even though she later suffered a miscarriage from accident-related stress.
